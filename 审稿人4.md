Comments and Suggestions for Authors

Thank you for the opportunity to review this manuscript. The paper addresses an important and timely problem: how to improve trustworthiness in AI-assisted oracle systems for blockchain settings, especially when correctness depends not only on agreement among agents but also on the reasoning process underlying their outputs. The idea of using perturbation-based “causal fingerprints” together with spectral analysis is interesting and potentially promising. However, in its current form, the manuscript has substantial weaknesses in scholarship, technical presentation, and reproducibility that prevent proper evaluation of its contribution. For these reasons, I cannot recommend acceptance at this time.

My main concerns are as follows:

1. Central claims are insufficiently grounded in the literature - The paper makes several strong claims about the limitations of existing oracle, consensus, and AI verification approaches, as well as the advantages of the proposed method. These claims are not adequately supported by prior work. In several places, the manuscript appears to overstate novelty or performance significance without demonstrating that comparable ideas, assumptions, or adjacent methods have been considered. A stronger and better documented literature foundation is needed, especially around:

    blockchain oracle security and consensus mechanisms,
    Byzantine fault tolerance and its assumptions,
    multi-agent AI verification,
    causal reasoning validation,
    adversarial robustness and perturbation analysis,
    spectral methods for anomaly or inconsistency detection.

At present, the paper does not sufficiently establish what is genuinely new, what is adapted from adjacent literature, and why the proposed mechanism should be considered a meaningful advance over existing alternatives.

2. Related work is underdeveloped and does not properly situate the contribution - The related work section does not position the study adequately within the state of the art. Some subsections contain too little literature to support the narrative, and the discussion remains descriptive rather than analytical. The manuscript should do more than list categories of prior work; it should explain:

    how prior studies address similar trust, verification, or adversarial issues,
    what exact gap remains unresolved,
    why existing methods are insufficient,
    how CFO differs methodologically and operationally from prior work.

Without this positioning, it is difficult to assess novelty, technical necessity, and significance.

3. System overview is incomplete - The system overview does not provide enough detail for a technical paper of this kind. In particular, the manuscript lacks a clear and explicit threat model. For a system claiming Byzantine detection and tolerance, this omission is critical. The paper should specify:

    the adversary’s capabilities,
    the trust assumptions,
    the communication assumptions,
    whether adversaries are static or adaptive,
    whether collusion is allowed,
    what information malicious agents can observe,
    what constitutes a successful attack,
    which properties CFO is intended to guarantee.

In addition, the implemented system is not described in sufficient detail. The paper should clearly explain the architecture, protocol flow, perturbation generation process, fingerprint construction, covariance computation, spectral analysis pipeline, decision thresholds, and how consensus is ultimately finalized on-chain or off-chain.

4. Experimental methodology is not sufficiently transparent - The abstract reports strong empirical results, including tolerance up to 40% Byzantine nodes and latency comparable to classical protocols. These are important claims, but the current presentation does not provide enough detail to evaluate them properly. The manuscript should clarify:

    the experimental setup,
    the datasets or task domains,
    the number and type of agents,
    how Byzantine behavior is instantiated,
    whether adversaries are coordinated,
    the baseline methods used for comparison,
    the statistical robustness of the results,
    sensitivity to parameter choices,
    failure cases and limitations.

The claim of exceeding the classical 33% bound is especially delicate and must be carefully framed. If the result is empirical and scenario-dependent rather than a formal protocol guarantee, this must be stated explicitly and precisely. Otherwise, the presentation risks overstating what has actually been demonstrated.

5. The paper lacks sufficient implementation detail for peer evaluation - The manuscript states that “The implementation and experimental data will be made publicly available upon acceptance.” This is not adequate for a paper whose main contribution rests on a new mechanism and empirical evaluation. Peer review must be able to assess the validity of the implementation and the credibility of the experimental claims. At minimum, reviewers need enough access to the implementation details, pseudocode, parameter settings, and experimental artifacts to evaluate correctness and reproducibility. If code or data cannot be released publicly during review, they should still be made available privately to reviewers or described in enough detail to permit meaningful scrutiny. As written, the paper asks reviewers to accept major claims without adequate evidence.

6. Reference quality and verification are a serious concern - I found the reference list problematic. Several references appear to be non-existent or are not traceable as cited. This is a major issue. A manuscript cannot build its contribution on literature claims if some references cannot be verified. The authors need to carefully audit the bibliography and ensure that every citation is accurate, complete, and corresponds to a real and relevant source. This issue raises concerns not only about scholarship quality but also about the reliability of the manuscript more broadly.

7. Stronger formalization is needed - The core mechanism would benefit from a more formal treatment. Important concepts such as “causal fingerprint,” “structural inconsistency,” and the decision logic based on spectral properties should be defined rigorously. It is currently unclear whether the method is intended as a heuristic detector, a probabilistic screening mechanism, or a consensus protocol with formal guarantees. The paper would be stronger if it clearly stated:

    the formal problem being solved,
    the assumptions required,
    the properties guaranteed,
    the boundary between empirical effectiveness and provable guarantees.

8. Limitations are not discussed sufficiently - The manuscript would benefit from a candid discussion of limitations. For example:

    How sensitive is the method to perturbation design?
    Can a strategic adversary adapt to the fingerprinting process?
    How does the method behave when honest agents are diverse but still valid in reasoning style?
    Could spectral divergence reflect model heterogeneity rather than Byzantine behavior?
    How does CFO scale with number of agents, task complexity, and on-chain cost constraints?

These issues are central to practical deployment and should be addressed explicitly.

Overall, the idea is interesting, and the paper touches on an important problem. However, the current manuscript is not yet at the level required for publication. The paper needs a substantially stronger literature foundation, a clear threat model, a fuller system and implementation description, verifiable references, and a reproducible evaluation package. I therefore recommend rejection in its current form, while encouraging you to revise and resubmit after major redevelopment.
