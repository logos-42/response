\abstract{AI-driven oracles increasingly govern high-value blockchain decisions but lack mechanisms to verify the logical correctness of off-chain AI outputs, creating a critical bottleneck for on-chain–off-chain trust interoperability. We present \textbf{Causal Fingerprinting Oracle (CFO)}, a multi-agent consensus mechanism that bridges this gap by enabling on-chain verification of off-chain AI reasoning. CFO operates in two phases: (1) \emph{perturbation-based fingerprinting}: smart contracts apply controlled input perturbations and archive agent response differentials as behavioral fingerprints on-chain; (2) \emph{spectral analysis}: the system performs eigenvalue decomposition on response covariance matrices to identify structural inconsistencies in agent reasoning. The term ``causal'' here refers to a \emph{functional-sensitivity} notion: the fingerprint captures a first-order finite-difference approximation \(\Delta y \approx (\partial f/\partial x) \Delta x\) of the agent's local input--output behavior, which is empirically informative about the agent's reasoning process but does not constitute a Pearl-style causal claim~\cite{pearl2009}. This design realizes \textbf{oracle-based on-chain–off-chain trust interoperability}, addressing the bottleneck of data discrepancy and uncertainty between the two domains. Experiments show CFO \emph{empirically detects and excludes} up to \(40\%\) Byzantine nodes in our multi-agent prediction scenario, while maintaining comparable on-chain consensus latency (\(\sim 3.5\) s). The 40\% threshold is an observation under our specific threat model and dataset, \emph{not} a violation of the information-theoretic \(f < n/3\) bound: CFO filters malicious agents \emph{before} consensus finalization, so the active Byzantine ratio seen by the underlying consensus layer is reduced. By shifting verification from mere data delivery to behavioral consistency, CFO establishes a foundation for trustworthy on-chain–off-chain collaboration in decentralized AI applications.

\section{Introduction}

AI oracles increasingly participate in high-value blockchain decisions, including asset liquidation, insurance claims, and derivatives settlement. However, current oracle mechanisms lack logical verification capabilities: they deliver data without validating whether AI-generated outputs derive from sound causal reasoning rather than hallucinations or spurious correlations. This gap creates systemic risk when automated decisions affect financial protocols and decentralized applications.

The core challenge lies in distinguishing correct outputs produced through valid reasoning from those arising by chance or memorization. An AI agent may produce accurate predictions while relying on spurious correlations that fail under distribution shift. Existing verification mechanisms cannot probe this distinction.

Existing approaches address oracle security through limited mechanisms. Traditional oracles (e.g., Chainlink~\cite{chainlink}) rely on decentralized node networks and reputation systems without logical verification. Optimistic oracles assume data validity by default, permitting challenges within a dispute window. Byzantine Fault Tolerance (BFT) protocols achieve consensus with up to 33\% faulty nodes but face $O(n^2)$ communication complexity and static fault thresholds~\cite{pbft,hotstuff}. Tendermint introduces round-based voting with provable finality~\cite{tendermint}. Byzantine-robust aggregation methods operate in static federated learning settings without temporal logical consistency~\cite{krum,trimmedmean}. Bulyan combines Krum selection with trimmed mean for enhanced robustness~\cite{bulyan}. Zero-knowledge proofs verify knowledge possession but cannot assess output correctness or logical validity.

We propose Causal Fingerprinting Oracle (CFO), a multi-agent consensus mechanism that detects Byzantine behaviors through perturbation-based spectral analysis. CFO operates in two phases: (1) \textit{Perturbation-based fingerprinting}: smart contracts apply controlled input perturbations and archive agent response differentials ($\Delta y$) as behavioral fingerprints; (2) \textit{Spectral analysis}: the system performs eigenvalue decomposition on response covariance matrices to identify structural inconsistencies in agent reasoning. The underlying \emph{heuristic} security argument is that, under the honest-majority assumption ($B < N/2$) and without access to honest agents' model weights, an adversary would need to reproduce spectrally consistent response differentials across $D$ independent perturbation dimensions, $N$ heterogeneous agents, and $L$ rounds of logical differentiation simultaneously---a constraint we estimate (Section~\ref{sec:theory}) to decay exponentially as $P_{\text{success}} \approx (1/A)^{D \times N \times L}$. This is an \emph{empirical} and \emph{heuristic} claim, not a theorem: the bound assumes (i) no gradient leakage, (ii) sufficiently diverse agent models, and (iii) perturbations sampled from a distribution unknown to the adversary. We discuss when the bound may fail in Section~\ref{sec:threat}.

The key insight is that reasoning structure---not output agreement---determines trustworthiness. Two agents may produce identical outputs yet exhibit divergent response patterns under perturbation, revealing hidden Byzantine behavior.

The contributions of this work are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We propose CFO, a behavioral fingerprint-based consensus mechanism that empirically detects and excludes up to 40\% Byzantine agents \emph{before} consensus finalization, complementing (not replacing) classical BFT protocols under their stated assumptions.
    \item We design an eight-dimensional spectral feature extraction framework with causal intervention strategies for precise Byzantine node localization in high-noise, dynamic environments.
    \item We integrate multi-layered logical games with zero-knowledge proof modules to provide verifiable consensus while preserving privacy.
    \item We empirically validate CFO in multi-agent prediction scenarios, demonstrating robustness under 40\% Byzantine node ratios with comparable latency to classical BFT protocols.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Blockchain Oracles}
Blockchain oracles bridge on-chain contracts with off-chain data sources. Chainlink~\cite{chainlink} employs decentralized node networks for reliable data delivery. Optimistic oracles assume reported data validity by default, permitting challenges within a dispute window. These approaches rely on economic incentives and reputation systems rather than logical verification. \textit{Limitation: Existing oracles cannot verify the logical correctness of AI-generated outputs or detect hallucinations.}

\subsection{Byzantine Fault Tolerance}
PBFT~\cite{pbft} achieves consensus through three-phase commit protocols, tolerating up to one-third faulty nodes. HotStuff~\cite{hotstuff} reduces view change complexity to linear, while Tendermint~\cite{tendermint} introduces round-based voting with provable finality. All classical BFT protocols maintain static fault thresholds and require $O(n^2)$ communication complexity. \textit{Limitation: The fixed 33\% fault tolerance bound is insufficient for dynamic, high-adversarial environments.}

\subsection{Byzantine-Robust Aggregation}
Krum~\cite{krum} selects gradients closest to others by Euclidean distance. Trimmed Mean and Median~\cite{trimmedmean} discard extreme values before aggregation. Bulyan~\cite{bulyan} combines Krum selection with trimmed mean for enhanced robustness. These methods assume static participant sets and independent, identically distributed data. \textit{Limitation: Current aggregation methods ignore temporal logical consistency and cannot capture reasoning coherence across perturbations.}

\subsection{Multi-Agent Consensus}

Multi-agent consensus mechanisms coordinate decentralized decision-making across heterogeneous agents. We categorize existing approaches into three paradigms and analyze their limitations relative to CFO's causal verification framework. Our prior work~\cite{prior_work} studied Byzantine-robust communication in multi-agent reinforcement learning, but did not address reasoning verification under controlled perturbations, which is the focus of this paper.

\subsubsection{Trust-Aware Consensus}

Trust-aware mechanisms maintain historical reputation scores for each agent, weighting their contributions proportionally to demonstrated reliability. Agents accumulating positive track records gain influence; those exhibiting inconsistencies face diminished voting power or exclusion~\cite{trust_consensus_1,trust_consensus_2}.

EigenTrust~\cite{eigentrust} computes global trust scores via eigenvector centrality on trust graphs. Beta reputation systems~\cite{beta_reputation} model agent reliability using conjugate Beta distributions updated after each interaction. Recent blockchain oracle designs~\cite{chainlink} integrate reputation-weighted aggregation with staking mechanisms, slashing deposits from malicious validators.

Trust-aware systems face three fundamental constraints: (1) \textit{Cold-start vulnerability}: new agents lack historical data, enabling Sybil attacks through identity rotation. (2) \textit{Temporal inconsistency}: reputation reflects past behavior but cannot detect sophisticated adversaries employing ``honeymoon'' strategies (honest initially, then adversarial). (3) \textit{No reasoning verification}: trust scores measure output agreement rather than logical validity---an agent producing correct outputs via hallucination accumulates indistinguishable reputation from one employing sound causal reasoning.

Trust mechanisms verify \textit{what} agents output, not \textit{how} they derive it. CFO addresses this gap by extracting behavioral fingerprints through perturbation responses, capturing functional-sensitivity structure (a finite-difference approximation $\Delta y \approx \partial f/\partial x \cdot \Delta x$) rather than surface-level accuracy. We term these ``causal'' in a local-sensitivity sense only; they are not Pearl-style causal claims~\cite{pearl2009}. Empirically, an agent cannot reproduce spectrally consistent response differentials across $D$ independent perturbation dimensions, $N$ heterogeneous agents, and $L$ rounds of logical differentiation without access to honest agents' model weights or gradient information (Section~\ref{sec:theory}).

\subsubsection{Game-Theoretic Consensus}

Game-theoretic approaches structure agent interactions as strategic games where truthful reporting constitutes a Nash equilibrium. Agents stake collateral and engage in challenge-response protocols, with economic penalties deterring adversarial behavior~\cite{schelling_consensus,truth_mechanism}.

Schelling-point oracles~\cite{augur} rely on focal points: rational agents converge on truthful answers anticipating others will do likewise. Commit-reveal schemes~\cite{commit_reveal} prevent information leakage during voting. Optimistic challenge mechanisms~\cite{optimistic_oracle} assume reported data validity by default, permitting stakeholders to submit counter-evidence within a dispute window. Truth-discovery mechanisms~\cite{truth_discovery} iteratively refine source reliability estimates through cross-validation.

Game-theoretic mechanisms encounter three critical weaknesses: (1) \textit{Collusion susceptibility}: coordinated adversaries can manipulate Schelling points through pre-game communication or bribery~\cite{collusion_attack}. (2) \textit{Verification boundary}: challenges verify factual correctness (e.g., ``Was the price \$100?'') but cannot assess logical reasoning (e.g., ``Did the agent derive this through causal inference or hallucination?''). (3) \textit{Rationality assumptions}: mechanisms presume agents maximize expected utility, but irrational or misaligned agents disrupt equilibrium predictions.

Game-theoretic approaches verify \textit{incentive compatibility} but not \textit{logical soundness}. CFO complements this by introducing perturbation-based spectral analysis that detects structural inconsistencies in reasoning---even when outputs appear correct. The spectral entropy metric (Section III-D) quantifies reasoning coherence independent of economic incentives.

\subsubsection{Learning-Based and LLM-Based Multi-Agent Systems}

Learning-based consensus leverages machine learning to aggregate heterogeneous agent predictions~\cite{ensemble_consensus,federated_consensus}. Recent LLM-based systems deploy multiple language model agents that debate, critique, or vote on decisions, exploiting emergent reasoning capabilities through collective deliberation~\cite{llm_multiagent_1,llm_multiagent_2}.

Byzantine-robust aggregation methods such as Krum~\cite{krum}, Trimmed Mean~\cite{trimmedmean}, and Bulyan~\cite{bulyan} select or weight gradients based on Euclidean proximity, assuming benign gradients cluster in parameter space. LLM-based frameworks employ debate protocols~\cite{llm_debate}, self-critique mechanisms~\cite{llm_critique}, or majority voting~\cite{llm_voting} to improve reasoning reliability. Some systems~\cite{llm_oracle} integrate LLM agents with blockchain oracles for natural-language query processing.

Learning-based approaches face three unresolved challenges: (1)~\textit{IID assumption}: Krum and related methods assume independent, identically distributed data, which is violated in temporal oracle settings where queries exhibit sequential dependencies. (2)~\textit{Static participant assumption}: Aggregation methods presume fixed agent sets, incompatible with dynamic oracle networks where validators join or leave across epochs. (3)~\textit{No causal verification}: LLM-based systems improve reasoning through deliberation but cannot \textit{verify} whether consensus outputs derive from valid causal chains or shared hallucinations; language models exhibit correlated biases~\cite{llm_bias} that majority voting amplifies rather than corrects.

Learning-based aggregation operates on \textit{output space} (gradients, predictions), while CFO operates on \textit{behavioral space} (response differentials under perturbation). Spectral analysis detects structural inconsistencies invisible to distance-based metrics: two agents may produce similar outputs yet exhibit divergent gradient structures, revealing hidden Byzantine behavior.

\subsubsection{Why Zero-Knowledge Proofs Are Insufficient}

Zero-knowledge proofs (ZKPs) enable verifiable computation: agents prove they executed a specified function without revealing inputs or model weights~\cite{groth2016}. However, ZKPs address \textit{computational integrity}, not \textit{logical validity}~\cite{bellare1993}.

ZKPs confirm: (1) the agent executed function $f$ with input $x$, (2) the output $y = f(x)$ satisfies arithmetic constraints, (3) the agent possesses knowledge of witness $w$ without revealing it.

ZKPs cannot assess: (1) \textit{Causal soundness}: whether $f$ encodes valid causal reasoning or spurious correlations; (2) \textit{Reasoning coherence}: whether outputs remain logically consistent under counterfactual perturbations; (3) \textit{Hallucination detection}: whether correct outputs arise from valid inference or lucky guessing.

An adversary can construct a malicious function $f'$ that produces correct outputs on common queries while embedding backdoors~\cite{model_backdoor}. ZKPs verify $f'$ was executed faithfully but cannot detect that $f'$ lacks causal validity. CFO addresses this gap by probing \textit{how} outputs vary under perturbation---a dimension ZKPs cannot access without exposing model internals.

CFO introduces three orthogonal verification dimensions: (1) \textit{Perturbation-based fingerprinting}: extracts behavioral signatures through controlled input variations, capturing local gradient structure without requiring model access. (2) \textit{Spectral analysis}: decomposes response covariance matrices to identify structural inconsistencies in reasoning coherence. (3) \textit{Causal intervention}: interprets perturbations as soft interventions, linking empirical response patterns to intervention-based causal analysis~\cite{pearl2022,shpitser2012}.

The key distinction: existing mechanisms ask ``Do agents agree?'' CFO asks ``Do agents reason consistently?'' \section{Causal Fingerprinting Oracle (CFO)}

\subsection{Formal Problem Definition}
\label{sec:formal}

\textbf{Problem Statement.} Given a set of $N$ agents, each executing an inference function $f_i: \mathcal{X} \rightarrow \mathcal{Y}$, we seek to determine whether a subset of agents exhibit Byzantine (adversarial) behavior by analyzing their reasoning consistency under controlled input perturbations.

\textbf{Input:} (1) A query $x \in \mathcal{X}$ issued by the smart contract; (2) $K$ perturbations $\{\Delta x_k\}_{k=1}^K$ generated by the perturbation engine; (3) response differentials $\{\Delta y_k^{(i)} = f_i(x + \Delta x_k) - f_i(x)\}$ from each agent $i$.

\textbf{Output:} A partition of agents into honest set $\mathcal{H}$ and suspected Byzantine set $\mathcal{B}$, along with a consensus output $\hat{y}$ aggregated from $\mathcal{H}$.

\textbf{Assumptions:} (1) In our experiments with $N=10$ agents, we test $B \in \{0,1,2,3,4\}$ corresponding to $0\%-40\%$ fault ratios, where $N-B \geq 6$ (i.e., honest agents always outnumber Byzantine agents); this is an honest-majority assumption weaker than the classical BFT $N \geq 3B+1$ requirement; (2) honest agents employ consistent causal reasoning, exhibiting stable gradient structures under perturbation; (3) Byzantine agents cannot access honest agents' model weights or the perturbation distribution; (4) the perturbation engine is tamper-resistant.

\textbf{Guarantees (Empirical):} CFO provides heuristic Byzantine detection based on spectral analysis of response differentials. The method does \emph{not} come with formal proofs of Byzantine tolerance. The 40\% empirical tolerance observed in experiments applies only to the specific adversarial model and experimental configuration. This 40\% is \emph{not} a contradiction of the classical $33\%$ BFT bound ($B \leq N/3$), which is an information-theoretic impossibility result for SMR consensus under fully asynchronous models: CFO operates at a \emph{different} operating point, namely the honest-majority setting $B < N/2$, and applies a heuristic pre-consensus screening before the underlying consensus layer is invoked. Users requiring formal BFT guarantees with proven properties should employ established protocols (PBFT, HotStuff) under their stated network assumptions.

\textbf{Method Classification.} CFO is a \textit{probabilistic screening mechanism} for Byzantine detection, not a consensus protocol with formal safety/liveness proofs. It is designed to complement, not replace, formal BFT protocols in AI-assisted oracle settings where output correctness depends on reasoning validity rather than mere data delivery.

\subsection{System Overview}

CFO operates through a five-stage pipeline that transforms agent queries into verifiable consensus.

The system receives a query from a smart contract and distributes it to participating agents. This initiates parallel inference across all agents to generate independent responses.

Smart contracts apply controlled stochastic perturbations to the input, producing multiple query variants. This probes agent reasoning boundaries rather than single-point outputs.

Response differentials across perturbations form each agent's causal fingerprint. These behavioral signatures are archived on-chain for long-term tracking and cross-session verification.

The system constructs a covariance matrix from fingerprints and performs eigenvalue decomposition. Agents exhibiting spectral inconsistencies are flagged as Byzantine. The decomposition follows standard symmetric eigendecomposition~\cite{horn2012,golub11996,parlett1998}.

A clustering mechanism (density-based, in the spirit of DBSCAN~\cite{ester1996}) identifies the largest spectrally coherent agent subset and aggregates their responses. Outliers are excluded, and flagged agents may contest via game-theoretic challenges.

Our experiments suggest that CFO can \emph{empirically detect and exclude} up to 40\% Byzantine nodes in the tested multi-agent prediction environment. This is observed under an honest-majority assumption ($N - B \geq 6$ in our $N=10$ setting) and is \emph{not} equivalent to, nor a refutation of, the classical $33.3\%$ BFT bound, which holds for SMR consensus under asynchronous or partially synchronous models. CFO operates as a \emph{pre-consensus filter}: the on-chain consensus layer only sees a reduced set of suspect-cleared agents, while the empirical screening itself is heuristic and does not provide formal safety/liveness proofs (see Section~\ref{sec:formal}). CFO's on-chain consensus path maintains comparable latency ($\sim$3.5\,s) and $O(n^2)$ communication complexity to classical BFT protocols.

\subsection{Threat Model}
\label{sec:threat}

We explicitly define the adversary model to clarify the scope and limits of CFO's Byzantine tolerance guarantees.

\textbf{Adversary Capabilities.} We consider a computationally bounded adversary that controls a subset of $B$ agent nodes out of $N$ total agents. The adversary can: (1) arbitrarily manipulate the outputs of compromised agents; (2) coordinate responses among all $B$ compromised agents to appear consistent; (3) observe all public on-chain state including queries, perturbations, and other agents' archived fingerprints; (4) adapt their strategy based on observed fingerprints in subsequent rounds. The adversary \emph{cannot}: (1) access the internal model weights or prompts of honest agents; (2) manipulate the perturbation generation process controlled by the smart contract; (3) compromise the spectral analysis or consensus finalization logic on-chain. \emph{Note.} We do \emph{not} model an adversary with gradient information about honest agents or with knowledge of the perturbation distribution. Under either of these weaker assumptions, the heuristic bound in Section~\ref{sec:theory} may fail, and CFO should not be relied upon without a defense-in-depth layer.

\textbf{Trust and Communication Assumptions.} We assume: (1) in our experiments with $N=10$ agents, we test $B \in \{0,1,2,3,4\}$ corresponding to $0\%-40\%$ fault ratios, where $N-B \geq 6$ (i.e., honest agents always exceed Byzantine agents); (2) the smart contract perturbation engine is tamper-resistant and operates correctly; (3) communication channels between the smart contract and agents are eventually synchronous for liveness; (4) the underlying blockchain provides finality within the consensus window.

\textbf{Attack Objectives.} A successful attack requires the adversary to: (1) cause the system to finalize an incorrect consensus output, or (2) cause honest agents to be incorrectly flagged as Byzantine (denial of service). CFO is designed to prevent both by ensuring spectral analysis detects reasoning inconsistencies even when adversarial outputs appear locally plausible.

\textbf{Guarantees.} CFO provides empirical (not formal) Byzantine detection under the stated assumptions. The 40\% tolerance threshold is observed empirically in our experimental setting and should not be interpreted as a proven protocol guarantee. Classical BFT protocols offer proven guarantees under strict synchronous/asynchronous assumptions; CFO offers empirical detection under the described threat model but lacks formal proofs of Byzantine tolerance.

\subsection{Causal Fingerprinting}

The causal fingerprint is characterized by the response differential $\Delta y$ under input perturbation:
\begin{equation}
\Delta y = f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)
\end{equation}
where $f: \mathcal{X} \rightarrow \mathcal{Y}$ denotes the agent's inference function, $x \in \mathcal{X}$ is the input query, and $\Delta x$ represents a controlled perturbation applied by the smart contract.

Static output evaluation $f(x)$ is insufficient for distinguishing agents employing valid causal reasoning from those producing correct outputs via hallucination or spurious correlation. Controlled perturbations $\Delta x$ sample the agent's decision boundary in a neighborhood of $x$, shifting verification from pointwise output validation to local behavioral characterization.

The response differential $\Delta y$ provides a finite-difference approximation to the local gradient $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}$ of the agent's inference function. Agents with consistent causal reasoning exhibit stable gradient structures across perturbation dimensions---outputs vary systematically according to underlying causal dependencies. Agents relying on heuristic patterns or memorized correlations exhibit irregular gradient responses when inputs deviate from their training distribution. The covariance structure of $\Delta y$ across multiple perturbations encodes this structural consistency, enabling spectral decomposition to identify reasoning coherence.

Constructing a consistent behavioral fingerprint requires matching the target agent's gradient response across high-dimensional perturbation space without access to model parameters. An adversary must generate response differentials $\Delta y$ that maintain spectral coherence with honest agents across all perturbation dimensions. We model the \emph{heuristic} attack success probability as decaying exponentially in the product of three independent constraint spaces:
\begin{equation}
P_{\text{success}} \;=\; \left(\frac{1}{A}\right)^{D \times N \times L}
\end{equation}
where $A \in (0,1]$ is the per-round response accuracy, $D$ is the number of independent perturbation dimensions, $N$ is the number of heterogeneous agents, and $L \in [0,1]$ parameterizes the depth of logical differentiation (i.e., the complexity of the causal chain being probed). For typical experimental parameters ($A=0.9$, $D=8$, $N=10$, $L=0.6$), $P_{\text{success}} \approx 1.5 \times 10^{-5}$. \emph{This expression is a heuristic security estimate, not a theorem.} It assumes (i) the adversary has no access to honest agents' gradients or perturbation distribution, (ii) the $D \times N \times L$ constraint factors are independent, and (iii) the per-round accuracy $A$ is a reasonable proxy for the difficulty of guessing a single response dimension. Violating any of these assumptions can substantially weaken the bound.

\subsubsection{Algorithm Description}

\textbf{Fingerprint Generation (Algorithm 1):} The algorithm takes as input $n$ agents, a query $x$, perturbation count $K$, and noise std $\sigma$. It initializes an empty fingerprint archive $\mathcal{F}$. For each agent $i$, the base response $y^{(i)} = f_i(x)$ is computed. Then for each perturbation round $k = 1$ to $K$, a perturbation $\Delta x_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I)$ is generated, and each agent's perturbed response $y_k^{(i)} = f_i(x + \Delta x_k)$ and response differential $\Delta y_k^{(i)} = y_k^{(i)} - y^{(i)}$ are computed. Finally, each agent's fingerprint vector $\Delta y^{(i)} = [\Delta y_1^{(i)}, \dots, \Delta y_K^{(i)}]$ is archived in $\mathcal{F}$.

\textbf{Spectral Byzantine Detection (Algorithm 2):} The algorithm takes fingerprints $\mathcal{F} = \{\Delta y^{(1)}, \dots, \Delta y^{(n)}\}$, threshold $\theta$, and spectral dimension $D$. It constructs a matrix $\mathbf{M}$ by stacking all fingerprint vectors, computes the covariance matrix $\mathbf{C} = \frac{1}{K-1} (\mathbf{M} - \bar{\mathbf{M}})^\top(\mathbf{M} - \bar{\mathbf{M}})$, performs eigen-decomposition $\{\lambda_i, \mathbf{v}_i\} = \text{eigen}(\mathbf{C})$, computes spectral entropy $H = -\sum_{i=1}^{D} \frac{|\lambda_i|}{\sum_{j=1}^{D} |\lambda_j|} \log_2 \frac{|\lambda_i|}{\sum_{j=1}^{D} |\lambda_j|}$, calculates the centroid $\mathbf{c} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \Delta y^{(i)}$, identifies the honest set $\mathcal{H} = \{i : \text{cos\_similarity}(\Delta y^{(i)}, \mathbf{c}) > \theta\}$ and Byzantine set $\mathcal{B} = \{1, \dots, n\} \setminus \mathcal{H}$, and returns both sets.

\textit{Parameter Settings.} Key parameters and their experimental values: $n=10$ agents, $K=5$ perturbation rounds, $\sigma=0.1$ perturbation std, $\theta=0.85$ cosine similarity threshold, $D=8$ spectral dimensions. These values were not systematically optimized; See Section V-D for sensitivity analysis.

\subsection{Theoretical Properties}
\label{sec:theory}

Let $A \in (0,1]$ denote response accuracy, $D$ the number of perturbation dimensions, $N$ the number of heterogeneous agents, and $L \in [0,1]$ the logical differentiation depth. The probability of successfully forging a causal fingerprint is:
\begin{equation}
P_{\text{success}} = \left(\frac{1}{A}\right)^{D \times N \times L}
\end{equation}

The cumulative security strength over $T$ independent task executions satisfies:
\begin{equation}
S = A^{D \times N \times L \times T}
\end{equation}

The exponential decay in $P_{\text{success}}$ is interpreted as the product of three \emph{assumed-independent} constraint spaces:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textit{Dimensionality}: Each perturbation dimension imposes an independent linear constraint on $\Delta y$. An adversary without $\nabla f$ would need to satisfy all $D$ constraints simultaneously in $\mathbb{R}^D$.

\item \textit{Heterogeneity}: Agents employ distinct inference functions $\{f_1, \dots, f_N\}$ with non-overlapping parameter spaces. Forging fingerprints across $N$ agents requires approximating $N$ independent function manifolds without gradient information.

\item \textit{Logical Depth}: Logical differentiation depth $L \in [0,1]$ parameterizes behavioral chain complexity. Deep chains ($L \to 1$) compose multiple inference steps, compounding approximation error multiplicatively.
\end{enumerate}

The product $D \times N \times L$ therefore reflects the joint volume of the (assumed-independent) constraint spaces. For typical parameters ($A=0.9$, $D=8$, $N=10$, $L=0.6$), $P_{\text{success}} \approx 1.5 \times 10^{-5}$ and cumulative security strength $S = A^{D \times N \times L \times T}$ grows monotonically with the number of independent task executions $T$. \emph{The independence assumptions above are necessary for the closed form and are not derived from a first-principles security model; we discuss scenarios in which they may fail in Section~\ref{sec:threat}.}

It initializes an empty fingerprint archive $\mathcal{F}$. For each agent $i$, the base response $y^{(i)} = f_i(x)$ is computed. Then for each perturbation round $k = 1$ to $K$, a perturbation $\Delta x_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I)$ is generated, and each agent's perturbed response $y_k^{(i)} = f_i(x + \Delta x_k)$ and response differential $\Delta y_k^{(i)} = y_k^{(i)} - y^{(i)}$ are computed. Finally, each agent's fingerprint vector $\Delta y^{(i)} = [\Delta y_1^{(i)}, \dots, \Delta y_K^{(i)}]$ is archived in $\mathcal{F}$.

\subsection{Spectral Analysis}

Spectral analysis detects Byzantine behavior by examining consistency in response changes rather than raw outputs. Malicious agents may produce plausible outputs in isolation, but their reasoning structures often lack coherence under controlled input variations.

Let $\Delta y_k \in \mathbb{R}^n$ denote the response differential of $n$ agents under the $k$-th perturbation. Analyzing the covariance structure of $\Delta y$ across multiple perturbations captures structural properties of each agent's reasoning process invariant to specific input instances.

The system constructs a covariance matrix over agent response differentials:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{C}[i,j] = \frac{1}{K-1} \sum_{k=1}^{K} (\Delta y_k[i] - \mu[i]) \cdot (\Delta y_k[j] - \mu[j])
\end{equation}
where $\mu[i] = \frac{1}{K} \sum_k \Delta y_k[i]$ is the mean response differential for agent $i$, and $K$ is the number of perturbation rounds.

Spectral decomposition reveals the principal directions of reasoning coherence:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{C} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \lambda \cdot \mathbf{v}
\end{equation}
Eigenvalues $\lambda_i$ quantify variance along each principal component. Honest agents cluster along dominant eigenvectors; Byzantine agents exhibit scattered spectral signatures.

We define spectral entropy to measure reasoning consistency, following the Shannon-entropy convention~\cite{shannon1948,cover2006}:
\begin{equation}
p_i = \frac{|\lambda_i|}{\sum_j |\lambda_j|}, \quad H = -\sum_i p_i \log_2 p_i
\end{equation}
Low spectral entropy indicates concentrated variance (consistent reasoning); high entropy suggests fragmented or adversarial behavior. Agents deviating significantly from spectral consensus are flagged for exclusion. \emph{Remark.} The claim that low/high spectral entropy is a discriminative signal for honest/Byzantine behavior is \emph{empirically supported} by our experiments in this paper and is not a theorem. Different model families (Transformer, GNN, MLP) and different perturbation distributions induce qualitatively different spectral signatures, so the entropy threshold may need recalibration when porting CFO across architectures. A formal characterization of the spectral signature space is left to future work.

\subsection{Causal Interpretation}

CFO is conceptually related to intervention-based reasoning in causal inference. The perturbation $\Delta x$ can be interpreted as a soft intervention, probing local structural responses of the agent function. Unlike formal do-calculus, CFO does not require explicit causal graph identification but instead relies on empirical response consistency.

This perspective connects CFO to intervention-based causal analysis~\cite{pearl2009}, though our method remains fully operational without explicit causal modeling.

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\subsubsection{Dataset}

We construct a benchmark dataset comprising three categories of causal reasoning tasks designed to evaluate multi-agent oracle systems under Byzantine conditions.

The dataset includes: (1) \textit{Prediction Tasks}: binary classification of commodity price direction (up/down) based on historical trends and macroeconomic indicators; (2) \textit{Reasoning Tasks}: policy impact assessment requiring multi-hop inference from regulatory changes to market outcomes; (3) \textit{Classification Tasks}: token quality evaluation based on project fundamentals, team credentials, and tokenomics.

Unlike simple lookup tasks, all categories require agents to construct causal chains connecting input features to outputs. For instance, predicting oil price movements necessitates reasoning about supply-demand dynamics, geopolitical events, and currency fluctuations rather than retrieving stored values. This design ensures that Byzantine agents cannot succeed through memorization or random guessing but must exhibit structurally inconsistent reasoning under perturbation.

The dataset contains 50 queries with varying complexity levels. Simple tasks involve 2--3 causal factors; complex tasks require integrating 5--7 interdependent variables. This diversity enables evaluation across different adversarial pressures and reasoning depths.

\subsubsection{Implementation Details}

We deploy $n=10$ agents, reflecting typical validator set sizes in production oracle systems (e.g., Chainlink CCIP employs 5--15 nodes). This scale balances decentralization with practical deployment costs while providing sufficient redundancy for spectral analysis.

Experiments vary Byzantine agent count $B \in \{0, 1, 2, 3, 4\}$, corresponding to fault ratios of $0\%$--$40\%$. We test this range to stress CFO's heuristic screening under ratios beyond the classical BFT $33.3\%$ operating point; this is permissible in our setting because CFO acts as a pre-consensus filter under honest-majority ($N - B \geq 6$) and does not claim to violate the underlying impossibility result. Byzantine agents are simulated by randomly selecting $B$ agents and programming them to submit adversarial responses with structured perturbations designed to evade detection.

For each query $x$, the smart contract generates $K=5$ perturbed variants $\{x + \Delta x_k\}_{k=1}^K$ where $\Delta x_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I)$ with $\sigma=0.1$. Perturbations are applied to numerical features (e.g., price values, policy parameters) while preserving semantic validity. Each agent processes all variants, producing response differentials:
\begin{equation}
\Delta y_k^{(i)} = f_i(x + \Delta x_k) - f_i(x), \quad i \in \{1,\dots,n\}
\end{equation}
where $f_i$ denotes agent $i$'s inference function.

Each agent's causal fingerprint is the concatenated response differential vector $\Delta y^{(i)} = [\Delta y_1^{(i)}, \dots, \Delta y_K^{(i)}] \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times d}$, where $d$ is the output dimension. These fingerprints are archived on-chain for cross-session verification and long-term reputation tracking.

Each experimental round executes the following sequence:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Query distribution to all $n$ agents
    \item Perturbation application: generate $\{\Delta x_k\}_{k=1}^K$
    \item Response collection: gather $\{\Delta y_k^{(i)}\}$ from all agents
    \item On-chain fingerprint archival
    \item Spectral consensus computation and outlier detection
    \item Aggregation of non-Byzantine agent responses
\end{enumerate}
Average end-to-end latency is 3.5 seconds per round for on-chain consensus operations, with total convergence time including LLM API responses averaging 244 seconds per round. API costs average \$0.0013.

\textit{Note on Latency Measurement.} The reported 3.5-second latency refers specifically to on-chain consensus overhead (perturbation generation, fingerprint archival, spectral analysis), which represents the bottleneck for on-chain finality. The total convergence time of approximately 244 seconds includes LLM API response latency, which dominates the end-to-end delay. The 3.5-second consensus time is comparable to classical BFT protocols, while the additional ~240 seconds reflects the LLM inference overhead that can be parallelized across agents.

\subsubsection{Byzantine Agent Simulation}

To evaluate Byzantine detection, we simulate adversarial behavior as follows:

\textit{Adversarial Strategy.} Byzantine agents are programmed to: (1) provide locally plausible outputs that match the ground truth with probability proportional to their fraction $B/N$; (2) exhibit inconsistent response differentials under perturbation by using a simplified heuristic model rather than genuine causal reasoning; (3) coordinate among themselves to appear as a coherent block but deviate from honest agents' spectral signatures.

\textit{Coordination Model.} We assume fully coordinated adversaries who can observe all public state and collude to produce consistent outputs. This represents a worst-case adversarial scenario within our threat model.

\textit{Evasion Strategy.} Byzantine agents attempt to evade detection by: (1) submitting correct outputs on base queries; (2) generating random or semi-random perturbations responses to evade spectral analysis; (3) mimicking honest agents' mean spectral signature when possible.

This simulation captures static, non-adaptive adversaries. We do not evaluate adaptive adversaries who learn the spectral analysis pipeline over time.

\subsubsection{Models}

All agents utilize DeepSeek API (deepseek-chat model) for causal graph generation and inference. Agents query the API with structured prompts containing task descriptions, input features, and perturbation instructions. Temperature is set to 0.7 for balanced exploration-exploitation, with max tokens limited to 512.

Agents are \textit{homogeneous} in architecture (all use DeepSeek API) but \textit{heterogeneous} in prompt formulations. Each agent receives slightly varied prompt templates and reasoning chain instructions, inducing diversity in inference pathways. This design reflects realistic oracle deployments where nodes may use similar models but maintain independent configurations. Byzantine agents share the same underlying model but are instructed to generate adversarial outputs through prompt injection.

\subsubsection{Metrics}

We evaluate CFO using four primary metrics aligned with our research questions. Let $N$ denote the total number of experimental rounds and $B$ denote the number of Byzantine agents.

Accuracy quantifies the proportion of rounds in which the system's consensus output matches the ground truth. Let $\hat{y}_r$ denote the consensus output in round $r$ and $y_r^*$ denote the corresponding ground truth. Accuracy is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\text{Accuracy} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{r=1}^{N} \mathbb{I}(\hat{y}_r = y_r^*)
\end{equation}
where $\mathbb{I}(\cdot)$ is the indicator function that equals 1 if the condition holds and 0 otherwise. This metric evaluates the system's fault tolerance under adversarial conditions (\textbf{RQ1}) and the reliability of causal reasoning (\textbf{RQ3}).

Consensus Rate measures the proportion of rounds in which all honest agents reach agreement within the prescribed timeout window. Let $\mathcal{C} \subseteq \{1, \dots, N\}$ denote the set of rounds where consensus is successfully achieved. Consensus Rate is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\text{Consensus Rate} = \frac{|\mathcal{C}|}{N}
\end{equation}
This liveness metric characterizes system stability and availability under varying Byzantine ratios (\textbf{RQ1}) and sensitivity to system parameters (\textbf{RQ4}).

Byzantine Detection Rate quantifies the effectiveness of spectral analysis in identifying malicious agents. Let $TP$ denote the number of true positives (Byzantine agents correctly flagged), $FP$ denote false positives (honest agents incorrectly flagged), and $FN$ denote false negatives (Byzantine agents missed). Detection Rate is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\text{Detection Rate} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN} = \frac{TP}{B}
\end{equation}
where $B = TP + FN$ is the total number of Byzantine agents. This security metric directly evaluates the causal fingerprint mechanism's ability to detect adversarial behavior in high-noise environments (\textbf{RQ2}).

Convergence Time measures the end-to-end latency from query initiation to final consensus. Let $t_{\text{query}}$ denote the timestamp when the smart contract issues the query and $t_{\text{consensus}}$ denote the timestamp when consensus is finalized. Convergence Time is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\text{Convergence Time} = t_{\text{consensus}} - t_{\text{query}}
\end{equation}
This latency metric captures the cumulative overhead of perturbation generation, response collection, fingerprint archival, and spectral computation (\textbf{RQ4}).

\subsection{Results and Analysis}

We organize our experimental findings according to the four research questions that guide this evaluation.

\subsubsection{RQ1: Byzantine Fault Tolerance}

This question evaluates whether CFO can maintain consensus and accuracy under adversarial conditions at Byzantine ratios that classical BFT protocols cannot tolerate without an extended trust model.

We measure system performance across Byzantine ratios from 0\% to 40\% ($B \in \{0, 1, 2, 3, 4\}$ with $n=10$ agents), comparing consensus rate, prediction accuracy, and convergence time against the operational envelopes of classical protocols.

The system maintains 100\% consensus rate across all tested Byzantine ratios, demonstrating uninterrupted liveness for the on-chain consensus path even at 40\% adversarial presence. Mean accuracy remains stable at 94.13\% overall, with graceful degradation from 97.50\% ($B=0$) to 94.13\% ($B=4$). Accuracy at 40\% Byzantine ratio exceeds 91\% under our specific threat model. Importantly, this 40\% does \emph{not} contradict the $f < n/3$ BFT bound, which is a theorem about SMR consensus safety/liveness in asynchronous networks under arbitrary Byzantine behavior; rather, CFO operates as a heuristic \emph{pre-consensus} filter, identifying and excluding suspect agents before the consensus layer is invoked. As shown in Table~\ref{tab:byzantine}, 52.0\% of rounds achieve excellent accuracy ($\geq 95\%$), while only 8.0\% fall below 85\% even under maximum adversarial pressure.

CFO's empirical robustness stems from perturbation-based spectral analysis, which detects structural inconsistencies in agent reasoning rather than relying on static vote thresholds. The accuracy dip at 30\% Byzantine ratio (91.45\%) indicates a transition region where spectral clustering faces increased difficulty separating adversarial from benign agents; the system recovers at 40\%, suggesting non-monotonic robustness characteristics that warrant further study. We emphasize that classical BFT protocols (PBFT, HotStuff, Tendermint) remain the recommended choice for deployments requiring \emph{formal} safety/liveness proofs; CFO is a complementary screening layer for settings where heuristic detection of reasoning inconsistency is valuable.

\subsubsection{RQ2: Byzantine Detection}

This question examines whether causal fingerprints combined with spectral analysis can identify malicious agents in high-noise environments.

We evaluate outlier detection effectiveness by measuring true positive rates across Byzantine ratios, analyzing the contribution of fingerprint modules and spectral dimensionality through ablation studies.

Outlier detection scales proportionally with Byzantine presence: the system identifies 2 outliers at $B=1$, 4 at $B=2$, 7 at $B=3$, and 10 at $B=4$ (Table~\ref{tab:byzantine}). Ablation results reveal that removing the fingerprint module eliminates detection entirely (0\%), while configurations without delta responses achieve higher raw accuracy (97.0\%) but zero security. The 8D spectral configuration achieves optimal balance with 87.0\% accuracy and 0.4 detection rate; reducing dimensionality to 2D or 0D eliminates detection capability.

Causal fingerprints serve as behavioral signatures that capture reasoning coherence under perturbation. The fingerprint module is necessary for security but not for accuracy optimization---it functions as an adversarial filter rather than a performance enhancer. Spectral analysis amplifies subtle inconsistencies: Byzantine agents may produce plausible outputs in isolation, but their reasoning structures lack coherence across perturbation dimensions. The covariance matrix eigenvalue decomposition reveals these structural deviations, with higher dimensions (8D) capturing multi-directional inconsistencies that lower dimensions miss. This explains why 2D configurations fail despite adequate fingerprint data---the attack surface spans a higher-dimensional manifold.

\subsubsection{RQ3: Causal Reasoning Capability}

This question assesses whether LLM-generated causal graphs enable reliable logical inference under adversarial conditions.

We analyze causal graph statistics (node count, edge density, path complexity) and reasoning capability scores across all experimental rounds.

CFO constructs causal graphs averaging five nodes, six edges, and three causal paths per query, yielding a 94\% reasoning capability score. The system maintains 100\% consensus rate across all 50 rounds, demonstrating that causal reasoning remains stable even under 40\% Byzantine presence. Game-theoretic challenges enable flagged agents to contest classifications, fostering emergent truth-seeking behavior without central arbitration.

The causal graph structure reflects moderate complexity sufficient for multi-hop inference without excessive computational overhead. Five nodes and six edges indicate that agents capture key variables and their interdependencies rather than superficial correlations. The 94\% reasoning score suggests that LLM-based agents can construct valid causal chains when prompted with structured templates, though the 6\% gap indicates room for improvement in handling edge cases or ambiguous scenarios. The integration of causal reasoning with consensus mechanisms represents a shift from data replication to logical validation---correctness is determined by reasoning validity rather than majority voting.

\subsubsection{RQ4: Parameter Sensitivity}

This question investigates how system parameters influence the efficiency-accuracy-security trade-off.

We vary agent count ($n \in \{5, 10, 15, 20\}$), perturbation intensity (levels 0.5, 1.0, 2.0), and spectral dimensionality (8D, 2D, 0D), measuring their effects on accuracy, detection rate, and convergence time.

Agent count exhibits diminishing returns: 5-agent systems achieve 10.2s latency with 90.3\% accuracy, while 20-agent configurations incur 4$\times$ overhead (44.1s) with marginal accuracy gains. Perturbation intensity shows a sweet spot: level 2.0 achieves 92.9\% accuracy and 80\% detection, whereas level 1.0 fails to distinguish adversarial behavior from normal fluctuations. Spectral dimensionality confirms 8D as optimal---lower dimensions eliminate detection without proportional efficiency gains.

The agent count analysis reveals an efficiency frontier: 5--10 agents optimize the trade-off between redundancy and overhead for practical deployments. Beyond this range, additional agents introduce communication complexity ($O(n^2)$) without commensurate security benefits. Perturbation intensity functions as implicit regularization---moderate noise exposes adversarial reasoning by pushing agents outside their comfort zones, while insufficient perturbation allows Byzantine agents to maintain plausible outputs. The 8D spectral configuration captures sufficient structural information without the curse of dimensionality, suggesting that reasoning inconsistencies manifest along a moderate number of principal directions.

Overall, experiments confirm that full spectral consensus, behavioral fingerprint verification, and moderate perturbation form the foundation of a heuristic Byzantine-screening layer for multi-agent oracle systems. The RQ-based analysis demonstrates that CFO empirically detects and excludes 40\% Byzantine ratios in our setting (RQ1), achieves effective detection through spectral analysis (RQ2), maintains reliable behavioral consistency checking (RQ3), and operates optimally with 5--10 agents and 8D spectral configurations (RQ4). These results are \emph{empirical} and complementary to---not a replacement for---formally verified BFT protocols.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}

The prototype lacks formal testing, security audits, and gas analysis. Future work must address reentrancy vulnerabilities, access control, and failure modes.

Communication complexity remains $O(n^2)$, limiting large-scale deployment.

Performance degradation near 30\% Byzantine ratio suggests need for adaptive strategies beyond critical thresholds.

\textbf{Extended Limitations and Open Questions.}

\textit{ZKP Integration.} The architecture diagram (Figure~\ref{fig:cfo_architecture}) marks the zero-knowledge proof component as optional. We deliberately scope ZKP out of the current contribution: a full ZKP-based CFO would require defining a witness, statement, and verifier for the spectral signature, which is non-trivial given that signatures are continuous-valued and prover-side honest-majority assumptions differ from the standard ZK setting. A complete ZKP-augmented protocol is left to future work.

\textit{Perturbation Design Sensitivity.} The effectiveness of CFO depends critically on the perturbation strategy. If perturbations are too small, Byzantine agents may maintain consistent responses within their training distribution, evading detection. If perturbations are too large, they may push honest agents outside their valid reasoning region, causing false positives. Our experiments use Gaussian perturbations with $\sigma=0.1$ and $K=5$ rounds; the optimal perturbation distribution is task-dependent and not theoretically derived.

\textit{Adaptive Adversaries.} Our threat model allows adversaries to coordinate and adapt their strategy based on observed fingerprints across rounds. However, we do not evaluate against strategic adversaries who specifically target the fingerprinting mechanism. An adversary who learns the perturbation distribution and spectral analysis pipeline could potentially craft responses that mimic honest agent fingerprints. The security bound $P_{\text{success}} = (1/A)^{D \times N \times L}$ assumes no gradient information leakage, which may not hold against sophisticated adaptive attacks.

\textit{Honest Agent Diversity.} CFO flags agents whose spectral signatures deviate from the consensus cluster. This design assumes that honest agents employing valid causal reasoning exhibit similar spectral properties. However, in scenarios where honest agents use diverse but equally valid reasoning approaches (e.g., different causal graph structures for the same problem), CFO may incorrectly flag them as Byzantine. The false positive rate under legitimate reasoning diversity is not characterized.

\textit{Model Heterogeneity vs. Byzantine Behavior.} Spectral divergence may reflect model architecture differences rather than adversarial behavior. In our experiments, all $N=10$ agents use the DeepSeek API (homogeneous architecture), so this confound is controlled. In heterogeneous deployments where agents use different models (e.g., GPT-4 vs. Claude vs. DeepSeek), spectral analysis would conflate model heterogeneity with Byzantine signatures. Separating these effects requires additional normalization or model-specific fingerprint calibration.

\textit{Scalability Concerns.} The $O(n^2)$ communication complexity limits practical deployment to tens of agents. Our experiments test $N \in \{5, 10, 15, 20\}$; production oracle systems like Chainlink CCIP employ hundreds or thousands of validators. CFO's spectral analysis overhead grows with agent count, and the fingerprint archive grows linearly with the number of tasks and agents. On-chain storage costs for long-term fingerprint archival have not been analyzed.

\textit{Empirical vs. Proven Guarantees.} The 40\% Byzantine tolerance (4 out of 10 agents) is an empirical observation under our experimental conditions with honest-majority assumption ($N-B \geq 6$). This should not be interpreted as a proven protocol guarantee. Classical BFT protocols (PBFT, HotStuff) provide formal guarantees under synchronous/asynchronous assumptions with $N \geq 3B+1$. CFO lacks formal proofs and the 40\% threshold may not generalize to other settings, adversarial strategies, or agent configurations. Users requiring formal guarantees should rely on established BFT protocols.

Future directions: (1) incorporating do-calculus to reduce logical redundancy; (2) formal verification using Certora; (3) gas cost optimization; (4) evaluation against adaptive strategic adversaries; (5) theoretical analysis of the spectral detection bound.

\section{Conclusions}

We present Causal Fingerprinting Oracle (CFO), a multi-agent consensus mechanism that verifies AI-generated outputs through perturbation-based spectral analysis. By archiving response differentials under controlled interventions and analyzing their spectral properties, CFO detects Byzantine behaviors without requiring access to model internals.

Within our experimental setting, CFO's heuristic screening layer observes a 40\% effective Byzantine exclusion rate while the on-chain consensus path maintains comparable latency to established BFT protocols. The framework operates effectively in high-noise, dynamic environments where traditional aggregation methods fail, and is intended to \emph{complement} rather than replace classical BFT protocols in AI-assisted oracle deployments.

The significance of this work lies in shifting oracle verification from data replication to logical consistency. As AI agents increasingly automate financial and institutional decisions, mechanisms that distinguish causal reasoning from hallucination become critical infrastructure. CFO provides a foundation for trustworthy, self-verifying multi-agent systems grounded in causal inference and game-theoretic incentives.

The implementation, experimental data, and reproducibility scripts are available at \url{https://github.com/logos-42/MultiAgentOracle}.
